Why Australian Tradies Lose Money on Blind Quotes

26 September 2026 · 5 min read

Quoting is supposed to be how you win work. For a lot of tradies, it's where the money quietly leaks out.

Not in a dramatic way. Not in a way that shows up on one invoice. It leaks out a bit at a time — a drive here, an hour there, a job that never comes back — until it adds up to real money by the end of the month.

The problem is the "blind" part: you're quoting a job you haven't properly seen.

The real cost of a blind quote

Walk through a single quote the old way.

A customer calls about a job. They describe it over the phone, badly. You can't tell if it's a small fix or a full day's work, so you drive out. Maybe it's twenty minutes away, maybe it's an hour. You look at the job, ask a few questions, and head back.

That's the trip. Now add the time to write the quote, send it, follow it up, and wait.

Here's the part that stings: none of that is billable. You don't get paid to look. You don't get paid to quote. You only get paid if you win the job — and if the job turns out to be smaller or messier than described, the quote you gave is suddenly wrong.

So a blind quote costs you three times:

  1. Time and fuel getting there. An hour of your day and a run of the ute you can't invoice.
  2. The jobs you didn't quote because you were out looking at this one. That's the hidden one, and it's usually the biggest.
  3. Accuracy. Phone descriptions are unreliable. You're pricing a job you haven't really seen, which means either you lose it on price or you win it and lose money doing it.

I'm not going to quote you an average wasted-trip figure, because I don't have a verified Australian source for it and I'm not going to invent one. Do the reasoning yourself: take how long a typical site visit takes all-in, times the number you do in a week, times what your hour is actually worth. Most tradies I've talked to find that number uncomfortable.

Why "just get better at phone screening" doesn't fix it

The usual advice is to ask better questions on the phone. That helps at the margin, but it doesn't solve the underlying problem: the customer's description is only as good as their words, and most people don't know the words.

"This pipe's leaking under the sink" can mean a five-minute fix or a rotted cabinet. No amount of clever questioning reliably separates those, because the customer genuinely doesn't know. They're not hiding anything. They just can't tell you what they're looking at.

You need eyes on the job. That's the actual fix.

Video changes the economics

A customer's phone has a camera on it. That's the whole insight.

If the customer records a short video of the job before you commit to anything, then:

  • You see the job before you drive out. Ten seconds of video tells you more than ten minutes of phone description.
  • You triage. The dead-end jobs get filtered out early, so you never leave the house for them.
  • You quote tighter. You've seen the space, the access, the condition. Your quote is based on evidence, not a guess.
  • You respond faster. A tradie who replies with a real answer in ten minutes beats one who books a site visit next Tuesday.

The tradie still does a proper look on the jobs that matter. Video doesn't replace that. It replaces the trips that were never worth making.

What to do this week

You don't need to rebuild how you work overnight. Start small:

  • Pick the jobs where a video would help most. Big jobs, vague descriptions, anything far away.
  • Send the link instead of booking the visit. See if the video tells you enough.
  • Notice the jobs you don't bother with. The near-misses you'd normally drive out to are where the time savings actually show up.
  • Keep a rough note of trips avoided. One week of honest counting will tell you more than any statistic I could give you.

If it saves you a handful of wasted trips a month, it's already worth it. If it doesn't, you've lost nothing.
