Why Australian Tradies Lose Money on Blind Quotes

26 September 2026 · 5 min read

Quoting is how you win work. For a lot of tradies, it's also where money leaks out.

It doesn't show up on one invoice. It leaks out a bit at a time: a drive here, an hour there, a job that never comes back. By the end of the month it adds up.

The problem is the blind part: you're quoting a job you haven't properly seen.

The real cost of a blind quote

Take a single quote, the old way.

A customer calls about a job. They describe it over the phone, badly. You can't tell if it's a small fix or a full day's work, so you drive out. Maybe it's twenty minutes away, maybe it's an hour. You look at the job, ask a few questions, and head back.

Then add the time to write the quote, send it, follow it up, and wait.

None of that is billable. You don't get paid to look, and you don't get paid to quote. You only get paid if you win the job, and if the job turns out to be smaller or messier than described, the quote you gave is wrong.

So a blind quote costs you three times:

  1. Time and fuel getting there. An hour of your day and a run in the ute you can't invoice.
  2. The jobs you didn't quote while you were out looking at this one. That's the hidden cost, and it's usually the biggest.
  3. Accuracy. Phone descriptions are unreliable, so you're pricing a job you haven't really seen. You either lose it on price or win it and lose money doing it.

I'm not going to quote you an average wasted-trip figure. I don't have a verified Australian source for it and I won't invent one. Work it out for your own week: how long a site visit takes all in, how many you do, and what your hour is worth.

Why "just get better at phone screening" doesn't fix it

Better questions on the phone help a little. They don't solve the problem: a customer's description is only as good as their words, and most people don't know the words.

"This pipe's leaking under the sink" can mean a five-minute fix or a rotted cabinet. No amount of clever questioning reliably separates those, because the customer genuinely doesn't know. They're not hiding anything. They just can't tell you what they're looking at.

You need eyes on the job. That's the actual fix.

Video changes the economics

Every customer has a camera in their pocket. If they record a short video before you commit to anything:

  • You see the job before you drive out. Ten seconds of video tells you more than ten minutes on the phone.
  • You triage. Dead-end jobs get filtered out before you leave the house.
  • You quote tighter, because you've seen the space, the access and the condition. Your quote is based on evidence, not a guess.
  • You respond faster. A tradie who replies with a real answer in ten minutes beats one who books a visit next Tuesday.

The tradie still does a proper look on the jobs that matter. Video doesn't replace that. It replaces the trips that were never worth making.

What to do this week

You don't have to change how you work overnight. Start small:

  • Pick the jobs where a video would help most: big jobs, vague descriptions, anything far away.
  • Send the link instead of booking the visit, and see if the video tells you enough.
  • Notice the jobs you don't bother with. The near-misses you'd normally drive out to are where the time savings show up.
  • Keep a rough note of trips avoided. One honest week will tell you more than any statistic I could give you.

If it saves you a handful of wasted trips a month, it's already worth it. If it doesn't, you've lost nothing.
