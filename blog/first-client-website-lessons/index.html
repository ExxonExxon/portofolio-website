What Building My First Client Website Taught Me

26 September 2026 · 5 min read

My first real client website was for a Melbourne trade business. They had money on the line and I had my reputation on it. I was 15 and I thought I was ready.

I could build the site. I wasn't ready for how much of the job had nothing to do with code.

The words matter more than the design

I spent the first part of the project on layout, colours and fonts, because that's the fun part and the part you can see.

The client didn't care about any of it. What they cared about: when a customer lands on the page, do they know what I do, where I work, and how to call me? Within five seconds?

For a small business site, that's the whole job.

Design only matters in how it delivers those three answers. A beautiful page with a hard-to-find phone number is worse than a plain one that answers everything. I redid the layout to answer those questions faster.

Design is how you make the answers easy to find. It isn't the thing itself.

Real clients don't hand you clean content

In every practice project I'd ever done, the content was magically there. A paragraph of text, a few photos, a logo, ready to drop in.

Real clients don't work like that. My first client gave me what they had, which was not much: a couple of photos taken on a phone, some details scattered across messages, and a real job to get back to. They didn't have a polished description of what they do, because they've never had to write one down.

I had to pull the site's copy out of them a question at a time. What do you do? Who's your usual customer? How far do you travel? What do you want people to do when they land here?

That's not a technical skill, but it's most of the work. Getting the right words and images out of someone who's busy and doesn't think their own job is worth explaining takes longer than building the site.

Simple is a feature you have to defend

Every site I build now starts with the same rule: the customer should be able to find you, understand you, and contact you, on a phone, in under a minute.

It sounds obvious, and it's hard to keep. There's always a temptation to add a gallery, a blog, an animation, a third font. Each one feels like more value. Most of them are just more things between the customer and the phone number.

On the first build I added too much, then fixed it by cutting. The site got better every time I removed something.

A small business site is a tool, not a portfolio piece. The best tool does its one job without getting in the way.

I learned to talk to adults who aren't my parents

Building a site for someone means asking them questions they haven't thought about, telling them when an idea is wrong, and chasing them for the photos they promised two weeks ago. I had to manage a project, a timeline and a person. I was 15 doing it.

The first few calls were awkward. I over-explained, I undersold, I said yes to things I should have pushed back on. By the end I'd learned that adults don't care how old I am if the work is good and I'm clear about what happens next.

That lesson was worth more than the website.

Why I do this for free now

After that build, I had to decide what to charge for the next one.

The obvious move was to build the portfolio, raise the price, and get paid properly. I went the other way.

I build and host all my client websites free while the client is with me. No upfront build fee, no hosting bill. Small changes after the build are a flat fee, and there's an optional monthly plan for ongoing care. The site itself costs nothing.

People find it suspicious. The reason is simple: I want a portfolio of real sites and real client feedback more than I want a few hundred dollars right now. Practice work doesn't teach me what a client with a real business to run does when a deadline slips or the photos aren't ready. A real client does.

So we're both getting something. They get a site that costs nothing to build or host. I get the experience and proof that I can actually deliver.

I'm upfront about it because pretending otherwise would be worse. I'm not doing free work out of charity. I'm buying the exact education I can't get any other way.
